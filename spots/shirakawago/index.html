Gifu · 白川郷

Shirakawa-go

A fairy-tale mountain village of steep thatched roofs

  • Best: January, February, October, November
  • Retro Japan
  • Scenic nature
  • Time: 2-3 hours for a relaxed walk and viewpoint visit
Plan a November route with this spotTours & tickets nearby (Klook)

Why go

Shirakawa-go is a mountain village in Gifu Prefecture famous for its gassho-zukuri farmhouses, whose steep thatched roofs resemble hands pressed together in prayer. The roofs are built that steeply to shed the region's heavy snow. Together with Gokayama in neighboring Toyama, the area was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1995. Rice fields, mountains and old farmhouses create a postcard scene in every season.

What to do

  • Stroll the village of Ogimachi among the gassho-zukuri houses
  • Climb to the Shiroyama viewpoint for the classic overhead view of the village
  • Step inside a farmhouse such as the Wada House to see the smoky wooden interior
  • Try local specialties such as Hida beef or soba at a village restaurant

Getting there

Direct highway buses run from Takayama (about 1 hour) and from Kanazawa (about 1.25 hours); some routes ask for reservations, so check ahead. There is no train station in the village.

When to visit

Winter brings deep snow and a special evening light-up on a few select dates, which usually requires advance reservations. Spring and autumn offer green fields or golden leaves, and weekday mornings are the quietest.

Photo tips

  • Shoot from the Shiroyama viewpoint in the morning or late afternoon for soft light on the roofs
  • Frame a single farmhouse with rice fields or a stream in front to show the scale of the roof
  • In winter, bring spare batteries and gloves and try a long exposure at dusk when the lights are on

Nearby

  • Gokayama's Suganuma and Ainokura, smaller and quieter gassho-style villages
  • Takayama Old Town with its Edo-period merchant streets
  • Kanazawa, easy to pair for a Hokuriku route

Good to know

Book your bus seats early in busy seasons and do not enter private homes or fields. Many houses are still lived in, so respect signs that mark private areas.
