Nikko Toshogu

Gold, lacquer and wild carvings hidden in an ancient cedar forest

  • Best: April, May, October, November
  • Retro Japan
  • Scenic nature
  • Time: 2-3 hours
Plan a November route with this spotTours & tickets nearby (Klook)

Why go

Nikko Toshogu is the lavish mausoleum shrine of Tokugawa Ieyasu, the founder of the Tokugawa shogunate. It was first built in the early 1600s and greatly expanded in the 1630s. Dozens of buildings are covered with gold leaf, lacquer and richly coloured carvings of animals, flowers and legends, and the whole complex is part of the UNESCO World Heritage 'Shrines and Temples of Nikko'. Famous works include the gorgeous Yomeimon gate and the original 'see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil' monkeys.

What to do

  • The Yomeimon gate, dazzling with hundreds of carvings
  • The 'see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil' monkeys on the sacred stable
  • The Sleeping Cat carving at the entrance to the inner shrine
  • The huge stone torii and five-storey pagoda at the approach

Getting there

From Tokyo take the Tobu Railway Limited Express from Asakusa to Tobu-Nikko Station (about 2 hours), or the JR line from Shinjuku to JR Nikko. From either station, take a bus or walk about 25-30 minutes to the shrine.

When to visit

Autumn foliage from mid-October to early November is spectacular, but also the most crowded. Weekday mornings are calmest; winter is quiet but cold and may bring snow.

Photo tips

  • Shoot Yomeimon from slightly to one side to catch its depth and carvings
  • Use the stone steps and cedar trunks as natural frames
  • Look for tight detail shots of the carvings, which are the real stars

Nearby

  • Rinno-ji temple and Futarasan Shrine
  • Shinkyo sacred red bridge
  • Lake Chuzenji and Kegon Falls

Good to know

Plenty of stairs and gravel mean comfortable shoes are a must, and it can be several degrees colder than Tokyo. Entry to the inner shrine areas may need separate tickets, so check the official site. Keep voices low and avoid photography where signs prohibit it.

Keep voices down inside the shrine halls, and don’t touch the carvings — some panels are original and centuries old.
